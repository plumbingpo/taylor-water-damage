Appliance Leak Water Damage in Taylor: What to Know

Most of the appliance leaks we see trace back to three machines. Washer supply hoses can burst and keep spraying at full pressure until the valve is closed. Dishwashers usually leak a little at a time, at the drain hose or door seal, hidden beneath the cabinets. A refrigerator ice maker line is a thin tube that can drip behind the unit for months. Cleanup is different for each of them.

On every Taylor job, the first step is moving the appliance and tracing the path the water took. In kitchens that usually means under the cabinets and beneath the flooring. In laundry rooms it means the wall behind the machine and the rooms next door, or the ceiling below if the laundry is upstairs. Then our team removes the standing water, meters the materials that soaked it up, and places equipment matched to the size of the damage.

Your First Steps

  1. Shut the Machine DownIf the outlet and the floor next to it are both dry, end the cycle and take the plug out of the wall. Otherwise, use the breaker to cut its power.
  2. Close the Supply ValveWashers have hot and cold valves on the wall behind them. Dishwashers shut off under the sink. Refrigerator lines have a small valve behind or below.
  3. Mop Up What You CanUse towels or a mop on whatever water is within reach, which keeps it from spreading beneath flooring and cabinets. Do not vacuum unless the machine is rated for wet and dry use.
  4. Keep the Door ClosedA dishwasher or washer that still holds water should stay closed. The entire load of water may spill across the floor if the door is opened mid-cycle.
  5. Document What FailedPhotograph the failed fitting, valve or hose, and hold on to the part itself. Your insurer and the appliance maker may both ask to see it.
Coverage

What About Insurance?

Homeowners policies often cover sudden and accidental events, and a burst hose or a machine overflowing with no warning is that kind of event. The policy typically pays for the water damage, not for the appliance or the failed part. Slow seepage from a dishwasher or ice maker line is a harder claim, because gradual leaks usually are not covered. How old the hose was and how it was maintained may also come up with some insurers. Before cleanup, take photos, save the failed part and read your policy.

Why It Happens

What Causes It

  • Rubber washing machine supply hoses that weaken with age and split under pressure.
  • A dishwasher with a failed pump seal, a cracked drain hose or a worn door gasket.
  • Standpipes that clog, or the drain hose from a washer coming loose from one.
  • Installation mistakes like a loose supply line connection or a dishwasher that does not sit level.
  • Inlet valves that stick open, so the dishwasher or washer keeps taking in water past the fill level.
If It Is Left

Where the Damage Spreads

Mold Behind Appliances

Hardly anyone looks at the dark, warm space behind a dishwasher or refrigerator. Long before it is discovered, a slow drip back there can be growing mold across the floor and wall.

Baseboards and Wall Bottoms

Drywall edges and baseboards pull up water that stands on a kitchen or laundry room floor. Often the worst of it is on the wall in back of the appliance, where it went unseen.

Adjoining Rooms

A washer hose can release water for hours. It passes under walls into hallways, closets and bedrooms, and soaks carpet and pad well away from the laundry room.

Flooring and Subfloor

Water slides beneath laminate, vinyl plank and hardwood. Cupping and swelling show up in the boards, and the subfloor can stay damp long after the surface appears dry.
